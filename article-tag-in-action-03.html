ARTICLE 03

The Irony of AI, Lean, and Value Stream Flow

Systems-First Leadership ⏱ 6 minutes

Article Details

Author Paul Poirier
Estimated Reading Time 6 minutes

There is a certain irony I cannot ignore.

I have spent more than two decades helping organizations fix the same underlying problem. Today, in the era of artificial intelligence and digital transformation, leaders are still struggling with the same performance failures I saw twenty years ago on factory floors. The industries have changed. The technology has changed. The language has changed. The root issue has not.

Performance is still determined by one thing: value stream flow.

When I began my career as a lean specialist, it became clear that every organization is governed by the same laws of movement. Work must flow. Information must flow. Decisions must flow. When that flow is broken, performance breaks with it.

Leaders often believe they have people problems, skill problems, or personal responsibility problems. In many cases, they have flow problems. Waste, friction, unclear handoffs, delayed decisions, and broken pathways prevent the system from moving the way it needs to move.

What surprises me is not that this happens. What surprises me is how often leaders fail to see it.

Leaders Cannot Improve What They Have Not Seen

Leaders rarely map their value streams. They rarely walk the flow. They rely on secondhand reports, dashboards, or assumptions about how the work should move instead of observing how it actually moves.

They assume people are not using tools correctly. They assume teams are resisting new processes. They assume performance is breaking down because someone is not trying hard enough. Then they go to the source and the truth becomes obvious.

The flow is broken. The waste is visible. The friction is everywhere.

One of the most important principles I learned early in my career was the need to go to Gemba. This means going to the place where the work is happening. When leaders walk the process, they see what reports cannot show them. They see how information moves from team to team. They see the delays. They see the interruptions. They see the handoffs that are unclear or inconsistent. They see where people are forced to compensate for system weaknesses.

This is how leaders learn to see. This is how they move from assumption to understanding. This is the moment when the path to performance becomes clear.

The Irony of Modern Transformation

The irony is that leaders intellectually understand the need for better systems, but many resist the discipline required to see the system.

They want the outcomes of strong value stream flow without doing the work of mapping, walking, and redesigning. They want results without visibility. They want adoption without clarity. They want performance without flow.

Today, I watch organizations repeat the same errors as the early days of lean, but now through the lens of artificial intelligence. KPMG reported that ninety-three percent of Canadian companies are adopting AI, while only two percent are seeing meaningful return on investment.1 This is not because AI is ineffective. It is because many value streams are ineffective.

Companies are layering new tools on top of broken pathways. They are increasing digital throughput without fixing the underlying waste. They are accelerating speed into fragmented systems that cannot support it.

This is the same pattern I saw in manufacturing. Leaders believed the new tool would solve the problem. But tools do not repair flow. Tools amplify whatever flow already exists.

If the flow is strong, tools can create exponential performance. If the flow is weak, tools magnify the confusion.

Adoption Follows Flow

There is also a human dimension to flow that leaders cannot overlook.

Adoption follows predictable patterns. Innovators lead. Early adopters follow. The early majority waits to see evidence. The late majority moves when others have already moved. Laggards change only when there is no other choice. These patterns are not unique to manufacturing or digital transformation. They are part of how people respond to change.

This is why training programs often fail. This is why new tools stall. This is why organizations talk about transformation for years without meaningful progress.

Leaders try to force adoption through messaging, pressure, or expectations. But adoption is not controlled by intention. It is controlled by clarity, evidence, and value stream design.

When the flow works, people can adopt the new system with confidence. When the flow is broken, people resist because the friction is too high.

The System Is Often the Obstacle

Leaders often focus on personal responsibility before they examine the path they are asking people to walk.

They ask people to move faster, but the system keeps tripping them. They push for more discipline, but the system produces confusion. They try to measure behavior, but the system produces inconsistency. When value stream flow is broken, people are forced to work harder just to stand still.

I have rarely seen organizations fail because their people lacked potential. I have repeatedly seen organizations fail because their flow was never made visible.

It does not matter whether the organization is deploying AI, redesigning operations, improving training, strengthening trust, or trying to scale performance. The system wins every time. It determines movement. It determines adoption. It determines whether people feel momentum or resistance.

The Competitive Advantage Is Visibility

The opportunity is clear.

Leaders who learn to see value stream flow gain an advantage few organizations ever achieve. They understand where the friction is. They know how to eliminate waste. They see how information should move. They design systems that work with human nature, not against it. They bridge the gap between intention and execution.

There is no need to wait for a major restructuring or another technology purchase. The path begins with something far simpler and far more powerful.

Go to Gemba. Walk the flow. Map the system. Ask where information slows, where decisions stall, and where people are compensating for broken pathways. Learn to see the reality of the work, not the assumptions leaders rely on.

The moment flow becomes visible, performance becomes solvable.

The irony of it all is that the world keeps chasing new solutions, but the fundamentals have not changed. Value stream flow determines whether a system works. It always has. Until leaders make flow the lens through which they design, decide, and lead, they will continue to repeat the same mistakes I witnessed twenty years ago.

Do Not Wait. Take the Following Action.

Choose one process in your organization. Go to where the work is happening. Walk the value stream from beginning to end. Identify one source of waste that is creating friction and commit to removing it within the next five days.

About the Author

Paul Poirier is the co-founder of AIM, BIG, and TAG, the unified systems-first ecosystem delivering fractional services that help leaders improve commercial growth, financial enablement, and human performance through clarity, alignment, and disciplined execution. His work integrates lean manufacturing, systems thinking, talent development, and operational design to help organizations operate with disciplined simplicity.

Before building this ecosystem with his business partners, Paul spent fourteen years with UPS, beginning as a driver and later serving as Canada's Call Center Manager and a Sales Manager in Ontario. After UPS, he became a lean manufacturing specialist, helping organizations across more than ten industries successfully transform how they operated with the support of Industry Canada, IRAP, and ACOA. These experiences shaped his conviction that systems and standards, not people, determine long-term performance and execution reliability.

Paul later developed the Trust Lens Framework to help leaders understand a critical truth about human performance. Even the best-designed systems require people who are willing to adopt them. The Trust Lens provides a simple way to identify individuals who are open to change and aligned with progress, versus those who create friction or undermine execution. This framework anchors his belief that talent performance is a system of both structure and behavior, and that alignment must be visible before performance can scale.

Today, Paul uses this multidisciplinary background to help distributed organizations and SMBs build systems that scale. His approach begins by creating focus, eliminating noise and ambiguity so leaders can execute with discipline and simplicity. By reducing complexity and establishing clear lines of information flow, Paul helps organizations create environments where people perform at their potential because the system enables them to stay focused, aligned, and consistent.

Paul believes unequivocally that systems, not people, determine performance. When leaders fix the system, results improve predictably and the team rises to its potential.
